GPS satellites don't track your phone. They just broadcast a steady radio signal that says, *"I am Satellite 14, here is my exact orbit, and the time right now is 12:00:00.000000000."*

Your phone listens to that timestamp, compares it against its own clock, multiplies the travel time by the speed of light, and gets its distance from the satellite.

That sounds straightforward until you check the numbers. Light travels roughly **300 meters in one microsecond**. Satellites carry rubidium and cesium atomic clocks accurate to a few nanoseconds, while your phone carries a cheap quartz oscillator that drifts by milliseconds. If your phone's clock is off by just one microsecond, every distance measurement is wrong by 300 meters, which is useless for turn-by-turn navigation.

The way GPS works around a terrible receiver clock is one of the cleanest math tricks in engineering.

## First, trilateration with a perfect clock

Start on a flat 2D map and pretend your phone's clock is magically synced to the satellites. Knowing your distance from one satellite puts you somewhere on a circle around it. Add a second satellite and the two circles intersect at **two** spots: your real location and a <Cue tone="crimson">mirror twin</Cue>. A third satellite breaks the tie and pins down your exact `(x, y)`.

## Now break the phone's clock

In reality, your phone's clock has some unknown offset `b`. Because you compare every satellite's timestamp against the **same** faulty local clock, every measured distance (called a *pseudorange*) is shifted by the exact same amount `b * c`.

If your clock is running `0.32` microseconds fast, all three circles grow by `96` meters at the same time. They no longer meet at a single point; instead, they pull apart and leave an empty <Cue tone="crimson">error zone</Cue> in the middle where no location satisfies all three circles.

Here is the trick. Since all three circles are wrong by the exact same unknown radius offset `b`, you don't have two unknowns (`x, y`) anymore; you have **three** (`x, y, b`). And three circles give you three equations.

Hit **Solve clock offset** below to run a real Gauss-Newton solver in your browser. It nudges `(x, y)` and shrinks or expands all three rings together until the mismatch drops to zero, recovering both your <Cue tone="sage">exact position</Cue> and your phone's hidden clock error to the nanosecond.

Real GPS simply moves this exact geometry up one dimension. In 3D space you have four unknowns (`x`, `y`, `z`, and your phone's clock bias `b`), which is why your phone needs a lock on at least **four** satellites to get a fix. As a side effect, the moment your phone solves for your location, it also turns its cheap quartz chip into a nanosecond-accurate atomic-synced clock for free.
