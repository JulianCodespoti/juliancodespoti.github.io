Every programmer hits `0.1 + 0.2 === 0.30000000000000004` in their first month, shrugs, blames floating-point math, and moves on. What gets skipped is that the exact same design decision also explains why old versions of Minecraft shattered into jagged terrain walls if you walked millions of blocks from spawn, and why adding `1` to a large float can silently return the original number unchanged.

The whole mystery collapses down to 16 bits.

## Fixed bits, repeating binary fractions

A 16-bit float (IEEE 754 `binary16`, the compact cousin of the 32-bit and 64-bit floats in your CPU and GPU) splits its 16 bits into three fields: 1 <Cue tone="copper">sign</Cue> bit, 5 <Cue tone="teal">exponent</Cue> bits that pick which power-of-two bucket you're in, and 10 <Cue tone="sage">mantissa</Cue> bits that place you inside that bucket.

In base 10, `1/3` never terminates (`0.33333...`) because 3 doesn't divide 10. In base 2, `1/10` has the exact same problem: `0.1` in binary is `0.00011001100110011...` repeating forever. Because a float only has a fixed number of mantissa bits, the hardware slices the repeating tail off and rounds to the nearest pattern that fits.

## What actually happens when you add `0.1 + 0.2`

To add two floating-point numbers, the CPU has to line up their decimal (binary) points first, just like adding `1.2 x 10^3 + 3.4 x 10^2` by hand. It shifts the smaller number's significand to the right until both numbers share the larger exponent.

Anything pushed past the right edge of the mantissa register hits a hard <Cue tone="crimson">precision wall</Cue>. For `0.1 + 0.2`, `0.1` shifts right by 1 bit, its repeating tail spills past the wall, and the final rounded sum lands just off `0.3` (`0.3000488` in `binary16`, or `0.30000000000000004` in `binary64`).

## Why the number line stretches out (and `x + 1 === x`)

That exponent trick lets a 16-bit number reach `65,504` while still representing numbers smaller than `0.0001`. The price is uneven spacing.

Every power-of-two interval (`[1, 2]`, `[2, 4]`, `[1024, 2048]`, `[2048, 4096]`) gets the **exact same number of mantissa ticks** (1,024 steps in `binary16`, or 8,388,608 steps in 32-bit `float`). Because each interval is twice as wide as the one before it, the gap between consecutive representable floats doubles every time you cross a power of two.

Between `1` and `2`, the ticks sit `0.00098` apart. By `1024`, those same 1,024 ticks are spaced `1.0` apart. Step past `2048`, and the gap widens to `2.0`, so `2048 + 1` lands in the void and rounds straight back to `2048`.

<NumberLineZoomFigure client:visible />

In 32-bit floats (`f32`), the exact same cliff arrives at `2^24 = 16,777,216`. Past that coordinate in a game engine, you can no longer nudge a vertex by 1 unit, physics steps snap in visible chunks, and noise functions receive stair-stepped coordinates that tear smooth terrain into jagged walls. Modern engines avoid the cliff either by switching world coordinates to 64-bit doubles (`2^53`) or by quietly recentering the world origin around the player so you never walk far enough from `0` for the ticks to spread apart.
