When you send a one-line follow-up to a coding agent on turn 18, it feels like sending a short message. It isn't. LLM APIs are stateless, so your harness sends the entire conversation from byte zero on every turn: system instructions, every tool schema, every file the agent read, every test log, and every reply so far.

That detail alone explains most of the weird economics of coding agents. In a real session, your own messages are usually less than 5% of the prompt; the rest is file reads, tool schemas (50 MCP tools at ~450 tokens each burns 22.5K tokens before turn 1 even starts), and raw test output that lingers in history for the rest of the chat.

Two non-obvious mechanics sit underneath all of this: how causal attention makes prefix caching strictly one-directional, and why cumulative input tokens form a 2D triangle across turns.

## Why one changed byte breaks the rest of the prompt

If inference servers had to recompute attention over 60,000 tokens from scratch on every turn, coding agents would be too slow and too expensive to use. Instead, providers cache the KV state of your prompt's leading prefix and let you reuse it at roughly a tenth of normal input price.

What catches people off guard is how fragile that cache is. In a decoder-only transformer, attention is strictly **causal**: every token's key and value vectors mathematically depend on every token to its left, and on nothing to its right.

That means changing a single byte near the front of the prompt (a ticking `Current time: 14:03:22` in the system prompt, or dynamically adding a tool schema mid-session) alters the KV state of that block and cascades through every single block after it, even though the later conversation text didn't change at all. Blocks to the left stay <Cue tone="sage">cached</Cue>; everything from the edited block rightward turns <Cue tone="crimson">invalid</Cue>.

Three practical harness habits fall straight out of that geometry. Keep the system prompt completely static (put live timestamps or git status in the latest user turn instead). Freeze the tool list for the life of a session. And treat the message array as append-only while you're working.

## The quadratic triangle (and why early compaction backfires)

Because every turn re-sends everything before it, cumulative input tokens across an N-turn session don't grow linearly. They fill a **2D triangle**: turn 1 sends 1 slice, turn 2 sends 2, turn 3 sends 3, and so on. Without caching, you pay full price for the entire area of that triangle.

Prefix caching changes the shape of your bill completely. Only the first turn and the thin <Cue tone="copper">diagonal edge</Cue> of brand-new tokens pay full price; the entire <Cue tone="sage">interior of the triangle</Cue> drops to 0.1x.

Now look at what happens when a harness tries to be clever and summarizes older turns every 4 steps to shrink the context window. Rewriting the middle of the conversation edits the front of the prompt, which turns that entire turn into a <Cue tone="crimson">cache miss</Cue>. Even though the rows after compaction are slightly shorter, paying full prefill on those reset rows costs **more** than just leaving the warm green triangle alone.

That is why good coding harnesses rarely summarize aggressively in the middle of a session. Capping noisy tool outputs *before* they ever enter history (truncating huge test logs to the head and tail, or offloading broad searches to a disposable subagent) keeps the triangle skinny without ever breaking the prefix cache, saving full compaction for when you're genuinely near the window limit.
